**Racial and Identity Profiling Act (RIPA) Dataset: Policing Data**

In 2015, the California State Legislature passed AB 955, also known as the Racial and Identity Profiling Act of 2015 (RIPA), which prohibits law enforcement from practicing racial and identity profiling. As part of the bill, each California state and local agency that employs peace officers is required to report all stop incident data to the Attorney General on an annual basis, which is then published through the [OpenJustice data portal](https://openjustice.doj.ca.gov/data) of the State of California Department of Justice. Agencies include all city and county law enforcement agencies, the California Highway Patrol, and public California college and university law enforcement agencies, but exclude probation officers and officers working in custodial settings, such as correctional officers. Although the eight largest agencies by number of sworn peace officers employed began collecting data in July 2018, all agencies have recorded data beginning in January 2022. With each row pertaining to one person at one stop, the data includes information about the circumstances of the stop and perceived characteristics about the identity of the individual stopped. Because racial identity is recorded based on the perception of the involved peace officer, this introduces an important measurement limitation to the analysis. Annual data and their corresponding Read Me files from July 1, 2018 to December 31, 2024 can be downloaded via zip files, which contain individual folders of data pertaining to each agency.

**Racial Justice Act via NorCal ACLU: Prosecutorial Data**

In 2020, the California State Legislature passed AB 2542, the Racial Justice Act (RJA), which bars the state from seeking convictions or imposing sentences that are influenced by a defendant’s race, ethnicity, or national origin. In order to hold prosecutors accountable, the public needs access to prosecutor data, but counties often make such data inaccessible to the general public. As such, the ACLU has partnered with BraunHagey & Borden LLP since the law’s 2020 enactment to gain access to this [data](https://www.aclunorcal.org/collections/racial-justice-act/?doc_type=data&doc_year=2023#search_documents), which can be filtered for by county, document type, relevant date, and keywords. The ACLU obtains this data by probing counties for their records via public records requests, introducing the possibility of censoring sensitive data, therefore skewing the results of my subsequent analysis. Given that each document comes from a different county, the formatting and data availability and granularity varies not only by county, but by document.
    
The Center for Policing Equity (CPE) downloaded and cleaned the 2021-2023 data from the Office of the Orange County District Attorney, which I utilized via Professor Grossman’s connection to Jason Tsui, a CPE employee. Each row represents one charge referred to the Orange County District Attorney’s Office (DA), so one person could have multiple rows for the same incident if they possess multiple charges, which could be grouped by source_defendant_id. One case could also involve multiple defendants, which can be grouped by source_case_id. Other information includes defendant identity characteristics, key dates (charging, filing, disposition, sentencing), charging information, and indicators for wobblers, enhancements, special circumstances, whether law enforcement referred the charge, and whether the DA formally filed each charge.

**2020 US Decennial Census Data**

Population data used to calculate per-capita stop, search, and hit rates were accessed from the 2020 US Decennial Census Redistricting Data (Public Law 94-171) through the US Census Bureau's public API. Specifically, race and ethnicity counts were used from Table P2, Hispanic or Latino, and Not Hispanic or Latino by Race, which provides population totals disaggregated by Hispanic ethnicity and racial categories. Data were extracted for Orange County (FIPS code 06059) using the Census API.
